# Lindy Effect

Use longevity as evidence when judging whether a software technology, idea, or
practice will remain useful. The longer a non-perishable thing has persisted,
the more reason to expect it to persist. Treat this as a useful prior, not a
guarantee or a substitute for checking fit.

## Apply the principle

1. Define the decision, constraints, and real problem. Separate immediate needs
   from long-term maintenance.
2. Compare established and newer options against those needs. Distinguish a
   durable foundation from a short-lived product, framework, or version.
3. Check evidence of longevity and current health. Consider sustained use,
   active maintenance, ecosystem support, compatibility, and ability to hire or
   train people. Research current facts when they affect the recommendation.
4. Weigh longevity against security, support status, performance, accessibility,
   integration, total cost, and migration risk.
5. Prefer the established option when it meets requirements and novelty adds no
   concrete value. Prefer a newer option when it solves a real problem and its
   benefits justify adoption risk.
6. For learning choices, favor fundamentals and skills that transfer across
   tools; include specialized skills when current work requires them.
7. State recommendation, evidence, tradeoffs, uncertainty, and what would change
   the decision.

## Guardrails

- Age alone does not prove quality, active support, security, or future demand.
- Survivorship can hide failed alternatives; lock-in can keep weak technology
  alive.
- Do not predict a technology's remaining lifetime from its age alone.
- Do not reject new technology automatically. Compare its demonstrated value,
  maturity, support, and reversibility with the established option.
- Keep recommendations specific to the user's constraints. Ask only when a
  missing requirement could change the decision.
